
Presented at the 2020 National Council on Measurement in Education virtual conference, this session covers new work on managing test-taking disengagement. Rapid guessing measures are widely used to flag disengaged item responses, but little is known about students who rapidly guess across several tests. Using achievement test data from six administrations over three years, the study asks whether rapid guessing is a stable trait-like behavior or depends mostly on situational variables. It applies a Latent State Trait (LST) model (Prenoveau et al., 2011), which splits each student's standing among peers at each time point into a trait factor free of situational or interactional effects and an occasion factor holding the situational or interactional part; when rank order stays fully consistent over time, all observed variance belongs to the trait factor. Rapid guessing proves fairly state-like next to achievement scores, which are fairly stable, and repeated rapid guessing is strongly associated with academic self-efficacy and self-management scores. The findings matter for detecting rapid guessing and limiting its effect on observed scores.

At the National Council on Measurement in Education 2020 virtual conference, Dr. Wise presents research on test disengagement and how scoring should deal with it. Achievement gaps are a central metric for U.S. education practice and policy, used to judge how effective and fair the system is at one time, across decades and as children move through school. They also inform wider social questions: male-female gaps feature in research on why women less often choose quantitative majors and related fields and earn less on average, and Black-White gaps track progress on long-standing inequities tied to socioeconomic disparity, segregation and discrimination. ### 7708. Are achievement gap estimates biased by differential student test effort? Putting an important policy metric to the test.

```yaml
url: "https://www.nwea.org/research/publication/are-achievement-gap-estimates-biased-by-differential-student-test-effort-putting-an-important-policy-metric-to-the-test/"
```

```yaml
date_source: page-meta
authors: ["Naomi Duran", "Susan Kowalski"]
document_url: null
doi: null
license: null
tags: [sel]
```

Achievement gaps drive many practices and policies meant to close them, and most gap studies assume that score differences between subgroups reflect differences in content mastery, which holds only if the groups try equally hard. Low test effort is known to be common and to pull scores down, but whether effort differs by subgroup, and so biases gap estimates, has not been shown. This study compares rates of rapid guessing, answering an item too quickly to have grasped its content, across subgroups including race and gender, then estimates gaps with and without controls for rapid guessing. Effort differs a lot: in later grades males rapidly guess almost twice as often as females, and Black students more often than White students. These differences mostly leave substantive readings of gaps unchanged, although basic conclusions on male-female gaps and about how gaps change as students advance can shift once effort is accounted for. The bias is hard to quantify, but the results are a reminder that scores measure achievement only as far as students are willing and able to show what they have learned. Why effort differs by subgroup merits further study and would likely help educators.

The study looks at how school poverty relates to school academic performance, measured both as achievement and as growth, to help educators, advocates and policymakers rethink how local schools are judged and to inform policy, for example on evaluating schools by achievement versus growth. Relying on achievement in federal and state school evaluation appears to bias judgments against schools serving vulnerable students, with possible harm to the most historically marginalized. Academic self-efficacy is linked to high school graduation and achievement, including math scores. Studies of math and self-efficacy over time usually do not model separate growth trajectories for the two, or how they relate within a person. Researchers have validated ways to use item response times from computer-based tests to flag likely disengaged responses, a long-recognized threat to inferences from achievement scores. ```

Computer-based tests yield growing metadata on how examinees respond, including solution strategies, concentration and speed, and item response time is among the most common.
